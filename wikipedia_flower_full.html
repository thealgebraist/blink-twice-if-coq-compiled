Flower

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
This article is about the reproductive structure. For other uses, see Flower (disambiguation).

A flower, sometimes known as a bloom or blossom, is the reproductive structure found in flowering plants (plants of the division Angiospermae). The biological function of a flower is to facilitate reproduction, usually by providing a mechanism for the union of sperm with eggs.

1.1 Perianth: Calyx and Corolla

Together, the calyx (sepals) and corolla (petals) constitute the perianth, the outer non-reproductive envelope of the flower. The calyx protects the developing floral tissues in the bud stage, while the pigmented corolla exhibits spectral absorption and UV reflection patterns designed to attract specialized animal pollinators.

1.2 Reproductive Organs: Androecium & Gynoecium

The fertile organs comprise the male androecium (stamens bearing pollen sacs) and the female gynoecium (one or more carpels enclosing ovules). Double fertilization within the ovary produces the diploid zygote and triploid nutritive endosperm.

2 Major Groups of Flowering Plants & Evolution

Flowering plants emerged during the early Cretaceous period, approximately 130 million years ago, rapidly diversifying to dominate terrestrial flora in an evolutionary radiation Charles Darwin famously termed an "abominable mystery". Living angiosperms comprise four primary lineages exhibiting distinct anatomical specializations.

Angiosperm Clade Distinguishing Traits Floral Architecture Global Diversity
Monocotyledonae Single cotyledon, parallel venation Trimerous perianth (multiples of 3) c. 70,000 species (Poaceae, Orchidaceae)
Eudicotyledonae Tricolpate pollen, reticulate venation Tetramerous / Pentamerous (4 or 5) c. 175,000 species (Asteraceae, Rosaceae)
Magnoliidae Ethereal oil cells, branched veins Spiral / Trimerous whorls, many stamens c. 10,000 species (Magnoliaceae, Lauraceae)
Basal Angiosperms Ancient relicts, laminar stamens Spiral arrangement, undifferentiated c. 200 species (Amborellales, Nymphaeales)

3 Pollination Syndromes & Coevolutionary Mutualisms

Angiosperm flowers have coevolved with diverse biotic and abiotic pollination vectors. Floral phenotypic suites—encompassing corolla morphology, pigment reflectance, olfactory chemistry, and nectar composition—correlate with specific pollinator guilds.

Pollinator Vector Syndrome Term Floral Adaptations & Visual Signals Representative Taxa
Apoidea (Bees) Melittophily Yellow / blue / UV guide patterns, sweet nectar Apple, Clover, Rosemary
Lepidoptera (Moths) Phalaenophily White corolla, nocturnal fragrance emission Tobacco, Evening primrose
Trochilidae (Birds) Ornithophily Vivid red tubular corolla, copious dilute nectar Fuchsia, Hibiscus, Sage
Anemophily (Wind) Abiotic Vector Reduced perianth, versatile pendulous anthers Grasses, Birches, Oaks
